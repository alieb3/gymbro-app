Rules that keep badges honest

  1. A badge comes from the anchor source’s own rating when it has one.
  2. A claim backed only by an educator is never above Emerging.
  3. An exercise choice with no study behind it is graded Unclear and explained by anatomy.
  4. Every claim stores its sources, badge and review date, so the AI coming later can answer only from claims.

Where the research disagrees

Do you need to train to failure?

Some studiesPooled studies find stopping short of failure gives similar strength and growth, with less fatigue.
Other studiesOther analyses find growth keeps rising the closer sets get to failure, while strength does not.
Why they may differPeople are poor at judging how many reps they have left, studies define "close to failure" differently, and most last under 12 weeks.
What we suggest for nowStop 1 to 3 reps short on most sets. Taking the last set of a safe exercise, like a machine or cable move, closer to failure is reasonable.

Does muscle keep growing with more weekly sets?

Some studiesThe ACSM review finds 10 or more sets a week helps growth, with returns fading near 18 to 20 sets.
Other studiesIn 34 trained men, 30 to 45 sets a week grew more muscle over 8 weeks than 6 to 9 sets, with no extra strength. The largest pooled analysis found no clear ceiling either: gains continued past 30 sets, but each step needed more sets.
Why they may differStudies count sets differently, high-volume trials are few and short, and swelling soon after training can look like growth on scans.
What we suggest for nowStart around 10 to 12 hard sets per muscle a week. Add sets for a lagging muscle only if you recover well.

Where the research disagrees

Is a stretch-focused range of motion better for growing muscle?

Some studiesTraining hamstrings in a stretched position (seated leg curl) grew more muscle than a shortened one (lying leg curl). Partial reps at long muscle lengths matched or beat full reps for the quads.
Other studiesPooled analyses and the largest trials in trained lifters find that half reps in the stretched range and full reps grow muscle about equally, and that longer vs shorter muscle lengths differ only a little.
Why they may differMost trials are small and short (8 to 12 weeks), many are in untrained people, and they measure different muscles and regions. The newest trials in trained lifters, one large and multi-site and one using MRI, found no difference.
What we suggest for nowUse a full range of motion and do not skip the stretched part of the lift.

Where the research disagrees

Does cardio get in the way of strength and size gains?

Some studiesAn older meta-analysis found cardio blunted power gains, and growth and strength too when the cardio was running. A newer one found trained lifters gained less leg strength when cardio shared the session.
Other studiesA newer meta-analysis found no interference with muscle size or maximal strength, only with explosive strength.
Why they may differThe amount and type of cardio, the gap between sessions and training experience all differ, and the ACSM review rates the evidence as undetermined.
What we suggest for nowDo both. If size or strength is your priority, keep hard cardio moderate, lift first when they share a session, and keep hard cardio a few hours away from leg training.

Do you need 10,000 steps a day?

Some studiesAcross 15 cohorts, the drop in risk of early death levelled off at 6,000 to 8,000 steps for people 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults.
Other studiesOther large studies found benefits for heart disease, cancer and mortality kept growing up to 10,000 steps and beyond.
Why they may differThey measure different outcomes and ages, and step counts are observational: active people differ in many ways besides steps.
What we suggest for nowAny increase helps. Aim for 7,000 to 10,000 a day; walking faster for part of it adds benefit.

Do crunches damage your spine?

Some studiesPig spines bent tens of thousands of times under load in a lab developed disc injuries, which led to advice to avoid repeated bending.
Other studiesLiving discs adapt to load, the lab cycle counts are far beyond any workout, and there is no human evidence that normal ab training causes injury.
Why they may differThe warning comes from animal tissue that cannot heal; the reassurance comes from reasoning about living tissue. Neither side has trials in people.
What we suggest for nowHealthy lifters can do loaded crunches in normal amounts. With current back pain, follow your clinician and use planks or rollouts instead.
