Questions people ask

Straight answers to what gets said in the gym, each with its evidence. Share one with a friend, or with your coach.

Will lifting make me bulky?

No. Muscle grows slowly; a bulky look takes years of training and eating for it.

Will sit-ups burn my belly fat?

No. Fat comes off the whole body; ab work builds the muscle underneath.

Should I skip training on my period?

No need. Go by how you feel that day, and take a lighter day when you need one.

If I stop training, will my muscle turn to fat?

No. Muscle and fat are different tissues; one session a week keeps most of the muscle.

Does soreness mean it was a good workout?

No. Soreness fades as your body adapts, while the muscle keeps growing.

Which supplements actually work?

Enough protein from food comes first. Then creatine monohydrate, 3 to 5 g a day, the best-studied supplement.

Are steroids really that harmful?

Yes. They can harm the heart and your own hormones, and some harm may not reverse.

How much weight will walking alone take off?

Slowly: at 150 minutes a week, about 2 to 3 kg over a few months. It helps most with keeping weight off.

Can I lift with high blood pressure?

Yes, if it is under control. Strength training lowers it; just never hold your breath.

On weight-loss injections, do I still need to train?

Yes. Part of the weight lost is muscle; lifting and enough protein help you keep it.

Open debates

Where the research disagrees

Do you need to train to failure?

Some studiesPooled studies find stopping short of failure gives similar strength and growth, with less fatigue.
Other studiesOther analyses find growth keeps rising the closer sets get to failure, while strength does not.
Why they may differPeople are poor at judging how many reps they have left, studies define "close to failure" differently, and most last under 12 weeks.
What we suggest for nowStop 1 to 3 reps short on most sets. Taking the last set of a safe exercise, like a machine or cable move, closer to failure is reasonable.

Does muscle keep growing with more weekly sets?

Some studiesThe ACSM review finds 10 or more sets a week helps growth, with returns fading near 18 to 20 sets.
Other studiesA trial in trained men found 30 or more sets a week grew more muscle than lower volumes, with no extra strength.
Why they may differStudies count sets differently, high-volume trials are few and short, and swelling soon after training can look like growth on scans.
What we suggest for nowStart around 10 to 12 hard sets per muscle a week. Add sets for a lagging muscle only if you recover well.

Is a stretch-focused range of motion better for growing muscle?

Some studiesTraining hamstrings in a stretched position (seated leg curl) grew more muscle than a shortened one (lying leg curl). Partial reps at long muscle lengths matched or beat full reps for the quads.
Other studiesPooled analyses find the differences between full range and stretch-focused partials are small and uncertain.
Why they may differMost trials are small, short (8 to 12 weeks), in untrained people, and measure different muscles and regions.
What we suggest for nowUse a full range of motion and do not skip the stretched part of the lift.

Does cardio get in the way of strength and size gains?

Some studiesAn earlier meta-analysis found cardio blunted growth, strength and power, most of all with running.
Other studiesA newer meta-analysis found no interference with muscle size or maximal strength, only with explosive strength.
Why they may differThe amount and type of cardio, the gap between sessions and training experience all differ, and the ACSM review rates the evidence as undetermined.
What we suggest for nowDo both. If size or strength is your priority, keep hard cardio moderate, prefer cycling, and separate it from leg sessions by a few hours.

Do you need 10,000 steps a day?

Some studiesAcross 15 cohorts, the drop in risk of early death levelled off at 6,000 to 8,000 steps for people 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults.
Other studiesOther large studies found benefits for heart disease, cancer and mortality kept growing up to 10,000 steps and beyond.
Why they may differThey measure different outcomes and ages, and step counts are observational: active people differ in many ways besides steps.
What we suggest for nowAny increase helps. Aim for 7,000 to 10,000 a day; walking faster for part of it adds benefit.

Some studiesIn athletes, losing about 0.7% a week kept more muscle and strength than losing 1.4% a week.
Other studiesIn a trial of adults with obesity, fast and gradual loss led to similar weight regain three years later.

Is BMI a good measure for you?

Some studiesBMI is cheap, simple and tracks health risk across whole populations, which is why guidelines use it.
Other studiesIt misreads muscular people, and risk rises at lower BMI in many Asian populations. Waist-to-height ratio predicts heart and metabolic risk better.
Why they may differBMI was built for groups, not individuals, and body build and fat distribution differ between people and populations.
What we suggest for nowUse BMI as a first check, and measure your waist too. If the two disagree, trust the waist more.

How fast should a new runner build up?

Some studiesIn 874 new runners followed for a year, those who added more than 30% to their weekly distance had more overuse injuries such as shin and knee pain, though the difference fell just short of significance.
Other studiesIn a trial of 532 new runners, a slower 13-week plan built on the "10% rule" had the same injury rate (about 1 in 5) as a standard 8-week plan.
Why they may differOne is a trial comparing two sensible plans; the other observed people who chose their own, sometimes big, jumps. Both suggest that very big jumps matter more than the exact percentage.
What we suggest for nowBuild up gradually, avoid big jumps, and repeat a week if it felt hard or something aches. The 10% rule is a habit, not a proven limit.

Does the exercise that "activates" a muscle most build it most?

Some studiesLab studies that measure muscle electrical activity (EMG) rank exercises, and many popular "best exercise" lists come from them.
Other studiesEMG readings are not a proven predictor of growth. In one trial the hip thrust showed more glute activity than the squat but grew the glutes no more; for the triceps, EMG and MRI growth pointed in opposite directions.
Why they may differEMG is one short session, skewed by electrode placement and nearby muscles, and says nothing about how long the muscle is under load, which seems to matter for growth.
What we suggest for nowGymbro ranks exercises by growth trials first and anatomy second. EMG only explains, it never decides.

Are big compound lifts enough, or do you need isolation moves too?

Some studiesIn beginners, lat pulldowns grew the arms about as much as curls, and pooled studies find little whole-muscle difference.
Other studiesSome muscles barely grow from compounds: squats missed the hamstrings and the rectus femoris, bench press missed the long head of the triceps, and curls beat rows for the biceps.
Why they may differIt depends on the muscle. Muscles that cross two joints often stay the same length in a compound lift, so they get little stretch under load. Studies also measure different spots and use beginners.
What we suggest for nowBuild on compound lifts, then add one isolation move for the hamstrings, the rectus femoris, the triceps long head, the side delts and the calves.

Is the hip thrust better than the squat for the glutes?

Some studiesThe hip thrust shows higher glute activity, and adding it to leg press and stiff-leg deadlifts grew the glutes more (with more total work).
Other studiesIn the one head-to-head MRI trial with equal sets, glute growth was similar, and the squat also grew the quads and adductors.
Why they may differActivity is not growth. The squat loads the glutes when they are stretched, the hip thrust when they are short. Only one equal-volume trial exists, in untrained people.
What we suggest for nowA deep squat or leg press is the base. Add the hip thrust as a second glute move, or as the main one if squats bother your back or knees.

Where the research disagrees

Do you need incline pressing for the upper chest?

Some studiesIn one 8-week trial, the incline press grew the upper chest more than the flat press, and EMG shifts toward the upper fibres up to about 30 to 45 degrees.
Other studiesThat trial was small, in untrained men training once a week, and the flat press also grew the upper chest. No trial in trained lifters exists.
Why they may differOne small trial and lab activity data agree in direction, but the size of the effect is unknown.
What we suggest for nowMake one of your chest presses an incline at 30 to 45 degrees. Decline pressing is not needed.

Where the research disagrees

Is the Nordic curl the best hamstring exercise?

Some studiesAdding Nordic curls roughly halved hamstring injuries across 15 studies of athletes.
Other studiesFor size, a slow seated leg curl grew the hamstrings more, and grew the long head about four times as much.
Why they may differThey answer different questions: injury rates in sport versus muscle growth. The injury studies vary a lot and showed no effect in the two female studies.
What we suggest for nowFor muscle, use the seated leg curl and a hip hinge. Add Nordics if you sprint, play football or have strained a hamstring before.

Where the research disagrees

Do crunches damage your spine?

Some studiesPig spines bent tens of thousands of times under load in a lab developed disc injuries, which led to advice to avoid repeated bending.
Other studiesLiving discs adapt to load, the lab cycle counts are far beyond any workout, and there is no human evidence that normal ab training causes injury.
Why they may differThe warning comes from animal tissue that cannot heal; the reassurance comes from reasoning about living tissue. Neither side has trials in people.
What we suggest for nowHealthy lifters can do loaded crunches in normal amounts. With current back pain, follow your clinician and use planks or rollouts instead.
